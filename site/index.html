  • paper_vintage.csv: the numbers exactly as published in the paper (data pulled 12 August 2026). The government revises past months, so later numbers for the same months can differ slightly.
  • weights.csv: the Census numbers the small-business shares come from.
  • raw_monthly.csv: value and tariffs for every country and month, as downloaded.

How it is calculated

  1. Every month the U.S. Census Bureau publishes, for each country, the dollar value of goods entering through the Los Angeles customs district and the tariffs charged on them.
  2. A separate Census report gives, for each country, the share of U.S. import dollars that belongs to companies with fewer than 500 employees: for example 39.4% for China and 17.8% for Japan.
  3. Each country's goods and tariffs are split by that share. For China, 39.4% goes into the small-importer part and 60.6% into the large-importer part.
  4. Adding up all countries gives two parts. The tariff rate of each part is its tariffs divided by its value. The gap is the small-importer rate minus the large-importer rate.
Small rate = Σ s × Duties  ÷  Σ s × Value
Large rate = Σ (1 − s) × Duties  ÷  Σ (1 − s) × Value
Gap        = Small rate − Large rate

Inside one country both parts get the same tariff rate. The gap comes only from which countries each part leans toward.

What this shows and what it does not

  • Exposure, not reaction. It shows how heavily the trade flows that small importers sit in were taxed. It cannot show whether small firms cut their imports more, switched suppliers or raised prices. That would need company-level data, which is not public.
  • A share of dollars, not of businesses. By count, firms under 500 employees are about 99% of U.S. importers; by import value their share is 4.5% to 48% depending on the country.
  • China drives most of the gap. The paper shows that without China the gap stays between −0.37 and +0.80 pp.
  • Not every country is included. Only countries with a published small-business share count (about 88% of the district's imports; see Coverage above).
  • A district, not just a port. The Los Angeles customs district includes LAX air cargo as well as the seaports, and it leans toward China more than the U.S. as a whole.
  • Shares are fixed. New Census shares apply only from the month their report is released; earlier months keep the shares that were in effect.
